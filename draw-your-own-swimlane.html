What you need

You don’t need software or a consultant to see where your team’s time goes. You need an afternoon, a wall and the people who do the work.

A swimlane is a drawing of a process with one horizontal lane for each person, role or system involved. Every step goes in the lane of whoever does it. That simple rule makes the expensive parts visible: the handoffs, the waiting and the information that gets typed in twice.

This guide is the same approach we use in our two-day analysis, cut down to something a small team can do on its own.

What you need

  1. 1
    About three hoursOne afternoon, with no meetings in the middle of it.
  2. 2
    Two to four peopleThe ones who actually do the work, not only the ones who manage it.
  3. 3
    Sticky notes in three coloursPlus a long wall, a whiteboard or a shared digital whiteboard.
  4. 4
    One recent, real caseA quote you sent last week, or an order that came in yesterday.

2 Pick one process

The most common mistake is drawing too much. “Our sales process” is not a process you can draw in an afternoon. Pick something with a clear start and a clear end, that happens at least every week, and that someone already complains about.

  • From enquiry to quote sent. A good first choice for most teams.
  • From order received to invoice sent. Usually full of retyping.
  • From new lead to first meeting. Usually full of waiting.

Write the start and the end on the wall before you do anything else. Whenever the discussion drifts outside them, point at the wall.

3 List the roles

Next, list everyone and everything that touches the process. Each one gets its own horizontal lane, drawn across the full width of the wall.

  • Put the customer on top. It keeps everyone honest about what the customer actually experiences.
  • Use roles, not names. “Seller” and “Back office”, so nobody feels the drawing is about them.
  • Give your systems a lane too. CRM, ERP, email. It’s the only way to see what people type into them by hand, and what the systems never do on their own.

Three to five lanes is normal. If you have more than seven, the process you picked is probably too big.

4 Follow one real case

Now take your real case and walk through it from the start. For every step, ask three questions: what happened next, who did it, and where did they do it? Write each step on a sticky note and put it in that person’s lane, left to right, with an arrow to the next one.

Write the rough number of minutes on each note as you go. Guesses are fine. You’re looking for the big numbers, not precision.

Draw what actually happened, not what is supposed to happen.

This is the rule that matters most. The side spreadsheet, the phone call to check a price, the reminder someone keeps in their own calendar: they all go on the wall. Those workarounds are usually exactly where the time goes.

5 Mark the waits and retyping

When the case is on the wall, go through it again with a different-coloured pen and mark three things:

  • Waiting. Anywhere the work sat still: waiting for an answer, an approval or someone to get back from holiday. Draw a small clock and write roughly how long.
  • Retyping. Anywhere information was copied from one place into another by hand. Draw two short parallel lines.
  • Rework. Anywhere something went backwards because it was wrong or incomplete. Draw a dashed arrow back to where it returned.

Then count the arrows that cross from one lane to another. Every crossing is a handoff, and every handoff is a chance for work to wait or get lost.

6 Read the drawing

Step back from the wall and look at it together. Some patterns jump out on their own. A few questions help with the rest:

  • Where do the marks cluster? Waiting and retyping usually bunch around one or two handoffs.
  • Which lane is the busiest? That person is probably your bottleneck, and probably tired.
  • Is the systems lane empty? Then your tools are storing information, not doing work.
  • Which steps does the customer never notice? Those are the first candidates to remove.

Pick one to three fixes. Each one usually does one of three things: removes a step, moves it into the systems lane, or merges two steps into one. For a rough sense of what each fix is worth, multiply the minutes on the notes by how often it happens in a week. We wrote more about that in how we estimate the time you’ll get back.

7 When to get help

Many teams can get a long way with just this afternoon. Take a photo of the wall, fix the obvious things, and draw it again in a month.

It’s worth bringing someone in from outside when:

  • Nobody agrees on what actually happens. A neutral person asking questions settles it faster.
  • The drawing gets political. It’s easier to say “this step wastes time” when the person saying it doesn’t work there.
  • You know what to fix, but not how to build it. Moving a step into the systems lane is where the technical work starts.
