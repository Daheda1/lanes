Introduction

When a new customer gets in touch, the first hour matters more than the next twenty-four. That is the finding of the most quoted study on how fast companies answer.

The study is from 2011, and you’ll find its numbers on almost every sales blog, often rounded, stretched or quoted without the fine print. I went back to what the researchers actually reported, because the real findings are strong enough on their own.

This article goes through what they did, what they found, what the study can’t tell you, and what it means for a company answering quote requests today.

2 What the researchers did

James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington published “The Short Life of Online Sales Leads” in Harvard Business Review in March 2011. It builds on two pieces of work.

  • An audit of 2,241 US companies. The researchers sent web enquiries to companies in industries such as financial services, cars, education, software, health care and professional services, and recorded how quickly, and whether, each one answered.
  • About 1.25 million real sales leads. Data from 42 US companies, 29 selling to consumers and 13 to businesses, showing how the chance of reaching a lead changed with the time it took to try.

3 What they found

First, how fast the 2,241 companies answered a new enquiry:

More than a third answered within the hour. Almost a quarter never answered at all. Among the companies that did answer within 30 days, the average time was 42 hours. That figure is often quoted as “the average response time”, but it leaves out every company that never replied.

Then the second finding, from the 1.25 million leads. Companies that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as those that tried an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as companies that waited 24 hours or more.

“Qualify” here means reaching a decision maker and having a meaningful conversation. It doesn’t mean winning the sale. But you can’t win a sale you never got to talk about.

The first hour is worth more than the next twenty-four.

4 What the study doesn’t tell you

A study is only useful if you know where its edges are. This one has a few.

  • It is American, and from 2011. Buyers today expect faster answers, if anything, but nobody has repeated the study in Denmark.
  • It is mostly about consumer leads. Two thirds of the companies in the lead data sold to consumers. A B2B buyer asking for a window quote behaves differently from someone filling in a car-loan form.
  • Qualifying is not winning. The study measures whether you got a real conversation, not whether you got the order.
  • Fast companies may be better at other things too. A company that answers within an hour probably also has better follow-up and better sellers. Speed is part of the difference, but the study can’t say how much.

None of that makes the result less interesting. It means you shouldn’t put “seven times more sales” in a business case. What the study shows clearly is that a lead cools fast, and that most companies are slower than they think.

5 What it means for a quote request

In a B2B company, the first hour usually isn’t lost to laziness. It’s lost because the request lands in a shared inbox, the person who can answer it is out with a customer, and the email is missing half of what’s needed to reply properly. So it waits until someone has time to do it all at once.

The fix is to split the first answer from the full answer.

  1. 1
    Within minutes: an answerAn automatic reply that confirms the request and asks for what you need to price it. I wrote about that in ask the questions before you quote.
  2. 2
    Within the hour: a personA seller who looks at the request, and calls or writes if it’s worth it. An automatic reply is not a conversation; it buys you time to have one.
  3. 3
    Within the promised time: the quoteWhen the answers are in, the quote goes out by the time the first reply promised.

The automatic reply on its own doesn’t win anything. What it does is make sure no request is silent for a weekend, and that the person who picks it up has what they need to act.

6 Measure your own first hour

Most teams have never measured their response time, and most guess it’s faster than it is. You can find out in an afternoon.

  1. 1
    Take your last 20 quote requestsFrom the shared inbox, or wherever they arrive.
  2. 2
    Note two times for eachWhen the email arrived, and when the customer first heard back from a person.
  3. 3
    Count how many were answered within the hourThen within the day, after a day, and never.
  4. 4
    Compare with the 37%Not as a target, but as a sense check. Then look at the slowest ones: where did they wait?

If the waiting turns out to be the problem, that’s exactly the kind of thing the two days are for. You can read how the method works.
