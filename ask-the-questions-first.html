Introduction

Most quotes aren’t slow because pricing is hard. They’re slow because the request arrives missing half of what you need to price it.

Every company that sends quotes knows what it needs before it can give a price. A wholesaler needs item numbers and quantities. A window maker needs drawings or measurements. A service company needs the address and the scope. The list is short, and it hardly ever changes.

Yet in most of the teams I meet, the first reply to a new request is typed by hand, by a busy seller, a day or two after the email arrived. And it asks for exactly the same things it asked for last week.

My suggestion is simple. Let a fixed, automatic reply ask those questions in the first minute.

2 Where the days go

Here is how a typical request moves when nobody asks the questions up front. Nothing in it is hard. It’s just slow, because every round trip waits for someone to have time.

The work in those steps is a few minutes. The waiting between them is days. Meanwhile the customer is waiting for a price, and may well have asked a competitor too. Research on response times suggests the first hour matters most; I go through it in the first hour.

3 You already know the questions

Look at the last ten quote requests your team handled, and at the first reply each one got. I’d be surprised if the replies weren’t nearly the same.

That list is the knowledge of your best seller, written down. For most companies it’s five to seven questions. Some examples:

  • A window and door maker. Drawings or measurements, how many openings, which product line, new build or renovation, delivery address and date.
  • A wholesaler. Item numbers or a description, quantities, delivery address, wanted delivery date, customer number if you have one.
  • A service company. The address, what needs doing, photos, how urgent it is, and the best time to visit.

These are examples. Your list will be different, and you already know what’s on it.

4 The fixed first reply

The idea is a single, fixed reply that goes out automatically when a new request arrives on your quote address. It thanks the customer, asks your questions, and says what happens next.

An example first reply, for a window maker

Thanks for your request. We’ve received it and will get back to you with a price.

To price it right the first time, we need a few things. If they’re already in your email, you don’t need to send them again.

  1. 1
    Drawings or measurementsAn architect’s drawing, a window schedule or a sketch with sizes.
  2. 2
    How many windows and doorsA rough number is fine.
  3. 3
    New build or renovation
  4. 4
    Delivery address and the week you need them
  5. 5
    Should we install them, or only deliver?

Just reply to this email. When we have these, you’ll get a quote within two working days. If it’s easier to talk, call us on [phone].

Notice what the reply does. It answers straight away, so the customer knows the request didn’t vanish. It asks only what you need to price the job. And it promises something specific, which you have to be able to keep.

This is a rule, not an AI project. Most mail systems can send a fixed reply to a shared address, and a simple form can do the same job. Start with the fixed version. It does most of the good, and it’s right every time.

5 Make it smarter: ask only what’s missing

A fixed reply has one weakness. It asks every customer the same questions, including the ones they already answered in their email. Ask someone for drawings they just attached, and the reply feels like nobody read it.

The next step fixes that. Before the reply goes out, the request is read and checked against your list. The reply then confirms what you already have, and asks only for the rest.

The same request, with a reply that read it first

Thanks for your request for the house on Strandvejen. We have your drawings and the delivery address.

To finish your quote, we only need two things:

  1. 1
    New build or renovation?
  2. 2
    Should we install the windows, or only deliver?

Just reply to this email with the answers.

That small change matters more than it looks. Two questions get answered; seven get put off until later. And when the customer can see that you read their email, they’re far more likely to reply the same day.

This is where AI earns its place, and only here. AI reads the email and the attachments and notes what’s there. Rules decide which questions are still open and what the reply says. If the reading is uncertain, the rule simply asks the question anyway. Asking once too often is harmless; asking too little isn’t.

6 Different questions for different products

Once the request is read, you also know what it’s about. That means the questions don’t have to be one list for everything. Each product line or service can have its own.

  • A window maker. Windows need sizes and opening type. Skylights also need the roof pitch. Doors need the lock and threshold. A renovation needs to know whether the old frames come out.
  • A wholesaler. Cable needs lengths and cross-section. Lighting needs the number of fittings and the room. A project order needs the delivery schedule.
  • A service company. A repair needs photos of the damage. A yearly service agreement needs the number of sites and the opening hours.

These are examples; your product lines will have their own. The point is that each customer gets a short list that fits what they asked for, not a long one that covers everything you sell. If the request doesn’t clearly fit one line, the reply falls back to the general questions.

This is also where the reply starts to pay off inside the company. The questions for each product line are what your most experienced people know by heart. Writing them down once means a new colleague gets complete requests from day one.

7 What to get right

  • Only new requests. Put it on the address where new quote requests arrive, not on a seller’s inbox in the middle of a conversation.
  • Only what you need to price. Every extra question makes it less likely the customer answers. If you can look it up yourself, don’t ask.
  • Make it easy to answer on a phone. Replying to the same email should always work. A form is a bonus, not a requirement.
  • Say who you are. Write it as your team, with a real phone number. Don’t pretend a person typed it.
  • Keep the promise. If the reply says two working days, the quote has to arrive in two working days. Pick a promise you can keep on a busy week.
  • Look at it again. Every few months, check which answers were still missing when you started the quote, and adjust the questions.

One more benefit is easy to miss. The customers who answer are usually the ones who mean it, so your team starts its day with complete requests from serious buyers.

8 Start this week

  1. 1
    Collect the last ten first repliesWhat did your team ask for, every time?
  2. 2
    Cut the list to what pricing needsFive to seven questions is plenty.
  3. 3
    Write the replyThank them, ask, and say what happens next. Read it aloud once.
  4. 4
    Switch it on for your quote addressOnly for new requests.
  5. 5
    Check after a monthAre requests more complete? Are quotes going out sooner?
  6. 6
    Then make it smarterSplit the questions by product line, and let the reply ask only for what’s missing.

It’s one of the smallest changes you can make to a sales process, and one of the few that helps from the very first email.
