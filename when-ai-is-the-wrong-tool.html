Introduction

Fixing the process is always better than adding AI. Put AI on top of a messy process, and you just get the mess faster.

Right now almost every tool a small sales team buys comes with AI built in, and almost every consultant suggests a chatbot, an agent or a copilot as the answer. Some of those are genuinely useful. Many are an expensive way of doing something a simple rule would do better.

I work with AI every day, and I still build most of my solutions without it. This is how I decide where it belongs, and where it doesn’t.

2 Predictable beats clever

A fixed process gives the same answer to the same input, every time. If a customer orders 40 units and your price list says 8% off from 25 units, the rule gives 8% off. Today, tomorrow and on the busiest day of the year.

An AI model gives the most likely answer. Most of the time that’s the right one. But “most of the time” is a very different promise from “every time”, and in a sales process the difference shows up as a wrong discount on a quote, an order booked to the wrong customer or a lead sent to someone on holiday.

Neither column is better. They’re good at different things, and most of the work is putting each step in the right lane.

3 Where rules win

Anything where you can write the rule down, and where a mistake costs money or trust, should be a fixed process. In small sales teams, that’s most of the work:

  • Pricing and discounts. Your price list and discount rules are already rules. Let them be rules.
  • Lead routing. Region, company size and product decide who gets the lead. No guessing needed.
  • Order entry. Once you know what was ordered, moving it into the ERP is pure mapping.
  • Approvals. Discounts above a limit go to the manager. Every time, not most times.
  • Follow-up schedules. A reminder three days after a quote is a timer, not an intelligence problem.

These are also the steps that are easiest to check, easiest to change when your prices change, and cheapest to run. That’s not a coincidence.

4 Where AI earns its place

AI is excellent at the part rules can’t do: turning messy human language into something structured. That’s a real problem in sales, because customers don’t send neat forms. They send emails, PDFs, half-finished spreadsheets and voicemails.

  • Reading what came in. Pulling the products, quantities and delivery date out of an order email.
  • Summarising. Turning a 40-minute call into five lines and three next steps.
  • Drafting. A first version of a reply or a proposal text, which a person then edits.
  • Sorting free text. Is this email a complaint, an order or a question?

The pattern that works is almost always the same. AI reads, rules decide, people approve.

Notice what AI doesn’t do in that drawing. It never decides the price, and it never sends anything to the customer on its own. If it misreads a line, the rules or the person catch it before it matters.

5 A four-question test

Before putting AI into any step of a process, I ask four questions.

Should this step use AI?

  1. 1
    Can you write the rule down?If yes, write the rule. It will be cheaper, faster and right every time.
  2. 2
    Is the input messy human language?Emails, PDFs, call notes. This is where AI genuinely helps.
  3. 3
    What does a mistake cost?If it’s more than a moment of someone’s time, a person checks before anything leaves the building.
  4. 4
    Will you need to explain the result later?To a customer, an auditor or a new colleague. If yes, the decision should sit in a rule you can point to.

AI reads. Rules decide. People approve.

6 What it means for your team

For a small sales team, the practical takeaway is simple. Start with the process, not the tool. Draw how the work actually moves, find the steps that waste time, and only then decide what each step needs. Most of the time it’s a rule. Sometimes it’s AI. Often it’s just deleting a step nobody needed.

The solutions that come out of that are less exciting to demo. They’re also cheaper to run, easier for your team to understand and change, and they keep working when nobody is watching them. Most of what I build is boring on purpose.
