Introduction

Everyone talks about saving hours. Very few teams know what one of those hours actually costs. Statistics Denmark does.

When I estimate the time a team would get back, the next question is always what that time is worth. You can guess, or you can look it up. Statistics Denmark publishes what people earn per hour, role by role, every year. The latest figures cover 2025.

This article goes through those numbers for the people who usually touch a sale: sellers, sales managers and the office staff around them. At the bottom is a calculator for your own team. Then it looks at what they mean for a sales team.

The role most sales teams are built around, the commercial sales representative, is also the biggest group: about 30,000 full-time employees in the statistic. The typical one earns 405 kr. per hour worked, or about 54,800 kr. a month.

3 Why the hour costs more than you think

Divide a monthly salary by the hours in a month, and you get a lower number than the one in the table. That isn’t a mistake. The two columns answer different questions.

  • Per month spreads the pay over every working hour in the year, including holidays and sick days.
  • Per hour worked counts only the hours someone actually spent working. Holiday pay, pension and bonuses are still in the pay, but the paid days off are not in the hours.

When you ask what an hour spent on a quote costs, you mean an hour of real work. So the right number is the one per hour worked.

Even that is on the low side for the company. It is what the employee earns. The employer also pays for things like insurance, equipment, the desk and the systems. Those costs vary too much between companies to put a number on, but they only push the cost up.

4 Where the hours go

Salesforce asks thousands of sales people every year how they spend their week. In the 2024 edition of its State of Sales report, they said about 70% of their time goes to things other than selling: admin, data entry, internal meetings, finding information, writing quotes.

That figure is people’s own estimate, not a measurement, and it covers many countries and industries. I wouldn’t use it for your team. But it agrees with what I see when we draw a process: the selling itself is a small part of the day, and a lot of the rest is moving information from one place to another.

An example, not a statistic

Say a seller spends one hour a day retyping, searching for information and chasing missing answers.

  3. 3

The hour a day is an assumption; your team might lose less, or a lot more. The point is the size of it. Small daily friction adds up to a real number over a year.

5 Saved time is not saved money

This is the part most estimates leave out. Giving a seller an hour back doesn’t put 405 kr. back in the bank. Their salary is the same either way.

The value is in what the hour is used for instead. An hour back is worth something when it turns into:

  • More customer time. More calls, more visits, more quotes followed up.
  • Faster answers. A quote out the same day instead of next week.
  • Growth without hiring. The same team handles more requests as the company grows.
  • Less overtime, and fewer evenings spent catching up on admin.

That’s why I always put the estimate next to what the time would be used for. The hourly cost tells you how big the opportunity is. It doesn’t tell you the result on its own.

6 How to use the numbers

  1. 1
    Use your own numbers if you have themYour payroll knows exactly what your team costs. The statistics are for when you don’t, or for a quick sense check.
  2. 2
    Use the median, not the averageA few very high salaries pull the average up. The median is closer to a typical person.
  3. 3
    Count hours worked, not hours paidAn hour spent on a quote is an hour of real work.
  4. 4
    Give a rangeTwo days of looking at a process are enough to see it clearly, not to time every step. We explain how in how we estimate the time you’ll get back.
