Introduction

About a third of the tasks in sales could be automated with tools that already exist. Only one in four companies has automated even one sales process.

Those two numbers come from McKinsey, and the gap between them is what this article is about. It isn’t a lack of technology. Most teams already pay for tools that could take over a large part of the routine.

What’s missing is usually simpler: knowing which third.

2 What McKinsey found

In “Sales automation: The key to boosting revenue and reducing costs”, published in May 2020, McKinsey brought together several pieces of its research.

  • About a third of tasks. Research by the McKinsey Global Institute found that approximately a third of sales and sales operations tasks can be easily automated with today’s technology.
  • One in four has started. Only one in four companies had automated at least one sales process.
  • What early adopters report. Companies that had automated reported efficiency gains of 10 to 15 percent, a sales uplift potential of up to 10 percent, and more satisfied customers.

Two things are worth knowing before you quote these numbers. First, they are from 2020, before the current wave of AI tools. If anything, more of the reading and writing work can be automated today than then. Second, the gains are what early adopters reported about themselves. They show what is possible, not what any one company will get.

3 Which third?

McKinsey’s number is an average across many tasks and companies. In a small B2B sales team, I find the automatable third is almost always the same kind of work: moving information from one place to another, and applying rules that are already written down somewhere.

Usually automatableNeeds a person
New requestsConfirming, sorting, asking for what’s missingJudging which ones are worth chasing
QuotesPricing from rules, building the documentExceptions, discounts outside the rules
OrdersReading the order, entering it in the ERPApproving before it goes through
CRMLogging calls, emails and ordersDeciding the next step with a customer
Follow-upReminders when a quote goes quietThe conversation itself

Notice the pattern. The left column is the work nobody joined a sales team to do. The right column is the job.

4 Why most haven’t started

McKinsey doesn’t say why three in four companies hadn’t automated anything. In the teams I meet, it usually comes down to the same few reasons.

  • The work is spread thin. The automatable tasks are a few minutes here and there, across many people. Nobody sees the total, so nobody owns it.
  • They start with a tool. A new CRM or an AI assistant goes in on top of the old process, and the routine moves to a new screen instead of disappearing.
  • Nobody has drawn the process. You can’t automate a step you can’t point to. Most processes live in people’s heads.
  • It feels like a big project. It doesn’t need to be. The first automation is often a single rule, like an automatic first reply.

5 A third of the tasks, not the people

It is tempting to read “a third of the tasks” as “a third fewer people”. That’s the wrong reading. McKinsey frames it the same way: the gains it describes come from giving sales people more time with customers, not from replacing them.

The tasks that can be automated are the small, repetitive ones spread across everyone’s day. Taking them away doesn’t remove a job. It gives the same people more time for the part that needs them: customers, judgement and the conversations that win orders. If you want to know what that time is worth, I went through the numbers in what an hour of sales time costs.

6 Find your third

You don’t need a strategy to find out where your third is. You need an afternoon and one process.

  1. 1
    Pick one processThe one that annoys people most. Quotes and orders are usually good candidates.
  2. 2
    Draw it as it actually runsEvery step, in the lane of whoever does it. Here’s how to draw your own swimlane.
  3. 3
    Mark each stepMoving information, applying a rule, or using judgement.
  4. 4
    Count the minutesHow long each moving or rule step takes, and how often it happens in a week.
  5. 5
    Start with the most frequent oneNot the biggest or the most impressive. The one that happens most often.
